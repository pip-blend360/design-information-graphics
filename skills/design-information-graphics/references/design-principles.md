# Design Principles

Create a visual argument from evidence, not a decorated chart. Start with what the audience must understand and the comparisons that support it.

- Preserve quantitative proportion and graphical integrity.
- Make important comparisons easy and place compared values close together.
- Integrate words, numbers, annotations, and marks within one composition.
- Prefer direct labels and precise encodings such as position and length.
- Provide necessary units, denominators, benchmarks, sources, and qualifications.
- Use small multiples when overlays become difficult to read.
- Use color to encode, group, or emphasize—not merely decorate.
- Remove elements that neither convey evidence nor help orientation.
- Keep useful complexity navigable rather than deleting it automatically.
- Never imply certainty or causality unsupported by the data.

Give every component a narrative role: orientation, evidence, explanation, qualification, or implication. Avoid panels that repeat the same claim. The story must arise from the ordering and annotation of evidence, not ornamental illustration.

Review by asking what the viewer should understand, which visible evidence supports it, whether proportions and comparisons are truthful, whether every element earns its place, whether caveats sit near the claims they qualify, and whether the evidence CSV can reproduce every visible value.
